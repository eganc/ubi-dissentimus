title: "Does it matter what they meant?"
type: value
surfaces_as:

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether to judge a decision, policy, or person by the intention behind it or by what actually resulted.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Results are what people live with. Good intentions don't feed anyone, and treating them as a defence is how bad policy survives review after review. Judge the outcome."

> "You can't run a society where people are condemned for consequences they couldn't have foreseen. Nobody would ever act. Intent is how we distinguish a mistake from wrongdoing, and it's the basis of every legal system we have."

**Where it shows up:** policy post-mortems, apologies and whether they count, workplace conflict, discipline with kids, historical figures, anything where "that's not what I meant" gets said.

**The test:**

1. **Was the outcome foreseeable at the time, with what was known then?** This is the question that does most of the work, and it's often answerable. Not "did they know" but "could they reasonably have known."
2. **What did they do when the result became clear?** This separates the two positions more cleanly than the original decision does. Almost everyone agrees that persisting after the outcome is visible is a different thing from causing it unknowingly.
3. **Are we deciding what to do next, or assigning blame for what happened?** These need different rules and get run together constantly. For the first, intent barely matters. For the second, it's central.
4. **Would you apply this standard to someone on your own side?** Asked in both directions. The answer is frequently no, and noticing that is worth more than winning the point.

**A worked example.** An argument about a policy that failed. Question 3 first: are we deciding whether to keep it, or whether the people who made it were fools? If it's the first, intent is irrelevant and the argument is much simpler. Half of these disputes are two people answering different questions.

**Where it ends:** if you agree on foreseeability and response and still differ, one of you thinks a functioning society has to protect good-faith actors from consequences they didn't intend, and the other thinks that protection is what lets harm continue. Both of those beliefs are held by reasonable people for reasons rooted in things they've seen.
