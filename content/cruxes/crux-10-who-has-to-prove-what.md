id: crux-10
title: "Who has to prove what?"
type: value
surfaces_as:
  - new regulations
  - drug approvals
  - changing a tradition
  - altering a family routine
  - product safety
  - whether an unproven claim gets treated as false or as open
---

**What you're really disagreeing about:** what the default is when nobody can prove anything, and therefore who loses the argument by failing to make their case.

**How each side would put it:**

> "The people proposing a change carry the burden. Things as they are have at least been tested by time. If you can't show the change is an improvement, we don't make it."

> "The current arrangement isn't neutral — it was chosen by someone, often for reasons that no longer apply. Treating it as the default that needs no defence just protects whoever benefits from it now."

**Where it shows up:** new regulations, drug approvals, changing a tradition, altering a family routine, product safety, whether an unproven claim gets treated as false or as open.

**The test:**

1. **Say the default out loud.** "If we can't resolve this, what happens?" Whoever the answer favours is not carrying the burden. Naming it is usually enough to reveal that you were arguing about the default, not the evidence.
2. **Is the current state actually the tested one?** Sometimes yes — it's been running for fifty years. Sometimes it was introduced recently with no more evidence than the proposal. Check which.
3. **What does being wrong cost in each direction?** Burden of proof is really a bet about which mistake you'd rather make. Say which mistake you're more afraid of.
4. **Do you apply this default to changes you like?** Everyone demands rigour from proposals they dislike and waves it through for ones they favour. Check yourself before you check them.

**A worked example.** Two people argue about a proposed rule, one demanding evidence it works, the other demanding evidence it's harmful. Question 1 exposes that they never disagreed about the evidence at all — there isn't any — they disagree about who that absence favours. That's the whole argument, and it took one question.

**Where it ends:** if you both name the default and still differ, one of you fears breaking something that works and the other fears preserving something that doesn't. That's a disposition toward change itself and it's remarkably stable across topics — which is useful to know about each other.
