**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether big organizations that produce information are more trustworthy than individual people who produce it — or less.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Big organizations get things wrong, but they have to show their work. There's a paper trail, other people check it, and if they're caught they have to publish a correction. One guy with an account has none of that."

> "Those checks look good on paper. In practice the organization protects itself, and the people paying its bills. An individual can say the true thing faster because they don't have to clear it with anyone."

**Where it shows up:** vaccines, government spending, nutrition advice, crime numbers, climate — basically any argument that ends with two people holding up two different phones.

**The test — ask these about *whichever* source you're leaning on:**

1. **Has it ever said "we got that wrong"?** Go find one. A newspaper has a corrections page. A person has old posts. If you can't find a single retraction from a source that's been running for years, that tells you something either way.
2. **Who pays for this, and what happens to them if they say the unpopular thing?** For an organization that's funders and advertisers. For an individual it's the audience — losing followers costs money too. Neither is free of this. The question is what it would cost *this* source specifically.
3. **When they changed their position, did they say so out loud, or did it just quietly change?**
4. **Can you get to the thing underneath?** The actual spending table, the actual study — not the write-up about it. If a source never links to what it's describing, note that.
5. **Does anyone independent get the same number?** If two sources differ, is it the number that differs, or only the framing around it?

**A worked example.** Two people argue about federal spending on a program. One quotes a news article, one quotes an account on X. Run question 4 on both: does either link to the actual budget line? Often neither does, and they're both quoting the same original figure with different adjectives attached. That ends the argument in about ninety seconds, and nobody had to lose.

**Where it ends:** if you run all five on both sources and still land in different places, you don't disagree about the facts — you disagree about which kind of failure worries you more. One of you is more afraid of an organization covering something up. The other is more afraid of one person with no one checking them. Both of those things happen. That's a real difference and it's not a mistake on either side. Stop there.
