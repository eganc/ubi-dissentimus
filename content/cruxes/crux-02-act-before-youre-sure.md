title: "Do you act before you're sure?"
type: value
surfaces_as:

**What you're really disagreeing about:** when the evidence is incomplete, is the safer move to act now, or to wait for better information?

**How each side would put it:**

> "If something might be causing serious harm, waiting for proof means the harm keeps happening while you wait. Certainty arrives too late to be useful. You act on the best available read and adjust."

> "Acting on incomplete information has its own costs, and they're usually invisible because nobody counts them. Half the things we were sure about ten years ago turned out wrong. Waiting is a decision too, and often the better one."

**Where it shows up:** vaccines and lockdowns, climate policy, drug approvals, food additives, screentime rules for kids, anything with the word "precautionary" in it.

**The test — both people answer, out loud:**

1. **Name a case where you'd go the other way.** If you're an act-now person, name something you'd wait on. If you're a wait person, name something you'd act on without proof. Anyone who can't do this isn't holding a principle, they're holding a side.
2. **What's your number?** Roughly how likely does the harm have to be before you'd move? You won't have a precise figure. But "if there's any chance at all" and "if it's more likely than not" are very different people, and most of us have never said which we are.
3. **Is it reversible?** Can we undo this if we're wrong, and at what cost? Most people's real rule turns out to be about reversibility, not probability — and that's a rule two people can often agree on even when their instincts differ.
4. **Who eats the cost of being wrong in each direction?** If we act and it was unnecessary, who pays? If we wait and it was serious, who pays? Frequently these are different people, and that's the actual disagreement in disguise.

**A worked example.** Two people argue about a policy. Ask question 3 first. If it turns out one is thinking about something easily undone and the other is thinking about something permanent, they were never arguing about the same decision — and they'll often swap positions once that's clear.

**Where it ends:** if you both answer honestly and still differ, one of you fears the harm of doing nothing more than the harm of doing the wrong thing, and one of you is the reverse. That's a disposition. It shows up in how you drive, invest, and parent, and it isn't going to change tonight.
