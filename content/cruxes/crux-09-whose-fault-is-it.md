**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether an outcome is best explained by the choices individuals made or by the conditions they were making them in.

**How each side would put it:**

> "People in the same situation get different results, which means choices matter. Explaining everything by circumstance takes away the one thing a person can actually control, and telling someone their effort is irrelevant is not kindness."

> "If a pattern holds across millions of people, it isn't millions of independent character flaws — it's the situation doing the work. Judging each case individually means never noticing the thing producing all of them."

**Where it shows up:** poverty, addiction, obesity, crime, school outcomes, business failure, your kid's screentime. Almost every social argument is secretly this one.

**The test:**

1. **Is the claim about one person or about a pattern?** Both can be true at once — someone made bad choices *and* the odds were arranged against them. Most fights here are one person talking about a case and the other about a rate.
2. **What changes if you change the conditions?** Where a place changed a policy and the numbers moved, that's evidence conditions matter. Where they didn't move, that's evidence too. This is checkable more often than people expect.
3. **What's the claim actually being used for?** "This person is responsible" and "this is where we should intervene" don't have to agree. You can hold someone accountable and still think the leverage is somewhere else.
4. **Same standard both directions?** Does the person emphasizing circumstance apply it to people they disapprove of? Does the person emphasizing choice apply it to their own successes? Answer honestly. Almost nobody passes this cleanly.

**A worked example.** An argument about why a group is struggling. Question 3 first: are you two disagreeing about who deserves blame, or about what would fix it? Those come apart immediately, and people who can't agree on the first often agree readily on the second.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that choices and conditions each contribute, you disagree about the mix, and about which error costs more — excusing someone who could have done better, or blaming someone who couldn't. Neither of you will produce a number for that.
