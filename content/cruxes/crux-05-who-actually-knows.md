**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether trained expertise or direct personal experience gives you a better read on a situation.

**How each side would put it:**

> "People who've studied something for twenty years see patterns across thousands of cases. Your experience is one data point, and you can't see what you don't know. That's not an insult — it's true of everyone including me."

> "Experts see the version of the problem that reaches them, filtered through categories they were taught. The person living it notices things that never make it into the data because nobody thought to measure them. Being trained in something also trains you not to see certain things."

**Where it shows up:** medicine and symptoms doctors dismiss, education policy vs. teachers, economic statistics vs. what groceries cost you, city planning, addiction, anything where someone says "with respect, I actually live here."

**The test — run on both:**

1. **What would this source *not* be in a position to notice?** Every vantage point has a blind spot. The expert can't see what never got recorded. The person living it can't see whether their case is typical. Both answers exist. Say them.
2. **Is this a claim about what happens, or about how often it happens?** Lived experience is strong evidence that something *can* happen and often the only evidence it happens at all. It's weak evidence about frequency. Most arguments here confuse the two.
3. **Has the expert view changed, and did it change because of people reporting experience?** Often yes — that's evidence the channel works. Sometimes it took decades. That's evidence it works slowly.
4. **Which complaint is this?** "They got it wrong" and "they were dismissive of me" are different grievances, and one sentence usually carries both. Someone leaves an appointment with a correct diagnosis and no follow-up questions asked, and later says *doctors don't know anything about this.* The diagnosis may have been right and the encounter may still have been bad. Ask which one is being claimed. If you argue the knowledge question when the grievance was about the encounter, you can win completely and change nothing — you answered something they weren't asking. Only the first complaint is what this crux can settle.

**A worked example.** Someone says a treatment didn't work for them; someone else quotes the trial data. Question 2 resolves it in a sentence — the trial says how often it works, the person says it didn't work for them, and both are true simultaneously. The actual disagreement is usually about what should happen next, not about either fact.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that expertise is better on frequency and experience is better on existence, and you still differ, you disagree about which error is worse: dismissing a real effect because it's rare, or chasing a false one because it's vivid. That's a temperament, and it's usually formed by something that happened to you.
