# Ubi Dissentimus — Crux Library

*Short form: Ubi.*

Recurring disagreements, turned into questions two people can actually check together.

## Format

- **What you're really disagreeing about** — plain-language version of the split. Neither side written as the mistake.
- **How each side would put it** — in their own words, the way they'd say it to a friend.
- **Where it shows up** — everyday arguments this hides inside.
- **The test** — questions you can both look up. Run them on *both* sides' sources, not just one.
- **Where it ends** — what to say if no question separates you.

---

## CRUX 01 — Who do you believe, and why?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether big organizations that produce information are more trustworthy than individual people who produce it — or less.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Big organizations get things wrong, but they have to show their work. There's a paper trail, other people check it, and if they're caught they have to publish a correction. One guy with an account has none of that."

> "Those checks look good on paper. In practice the organization protects itself, and the people paying its bills. An individual can say the true thing faster because they don't have to clear it with anyone."

**Where it shows up:** vaccines, government spending, nutrition advice, crime numbers, climate — basically any argument that ends with two people holding up two different phones.

**The test — ask these about *whichever* source you're leaning on:**

1. **Has it ever said "we got that wrong"?** Go find one. A newspaper has a corrections page. A person has old posts. If you can't find a single retraction from a source that's been running for years, that tells you something either way.
2. **Who pays for this, and what happens to them if they say the unpopular thing?** For an organization that's funders and advertisers. For an individual it's the audience — losing followers costs money too. Neither is free of this. The question is what it would cost *this* source specifically.
3. **When they changed their position, did they say so out loud, or did it just quietly change?**
4. **Can you get to the thing underneath?** The actual spending table, the actual study — not the write-up about it. If a source never links to what it's describing, note that.
5. **Does anyone independent get the same number?** If two sources differ, is it the number that differs, or only the framing around it?

**A worked example.** Two people argue about federal spending on a program. One quotes a news article, one quotes an account on X. Run question 4 on both: does either link to the actual budget line? Often neither does, and they're both quoting the same original figure with different adjectives attached. That ends the argument in about ninety seconds, and nobody had to lose.

**Where it ends:** if you run all five on both sources and still land in different places, you don't disagree about the facts — you disagree about which kind of failure worries you more. One of you is more afraid of an organization covering something up. The other is more afraid of one person with no one checking them. Both of those things happen. That's a real difference and it's not a mistake on either side. Stop there.

## A note on two kinds of crux

Crux 01 is an **evidence crux** — the two sides could in principle look something up. The three below are **value cruxes**. No amount of looking things up settles them, because both people can accept every fact and still disagree.

Value cruxes get a different kind of test. Not "what would we observe," but "where's your line, and do you apply it consistently?" The goal is the same — find the seam — but you find it by locating each person's threshold rather than by checking a source.

Getting this wrong is the main way a session goes bad: run a value disagreement through evidence questions and you'll manufacture a fake source-trust seam that everyone nods at and nobody actually believes.

## CRUX 02 — Do you act before you're sure?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** when the evidence is incomplete, is the safer move to act now, or to wait for better information?

**How each side would put it:**

> "If something might be causing serious harm, waiting for proof means the harm keeps happening while you wait. Certainty arrives too late to be useful. You act on the best available read and adjust."

> "Acting on incomplete information has its own costs, and they're usually invisible because nobody counts them. Half the things we were sure about ten years ago turned out wrong. Waiting is a decision too, and often the better one."

**Where it shows up:** vaccines and lockdowns, climate policy, drug approvals, food additives, screentime rules for kids, anything with the word "precautionary" in it.

**The test — both people answer, out loud:**

1. **Name a case where you'd go the other way.** If you're an act-now person, name something you'd wait on. If you're a wait person, name something you'd act on without proof. Anyone who can't do this isn't holding a principle, they're holding a side.
2. **What's your number?** Roughly how likely does the harm have to be before you'd move? You won't have a precise figure. But "if there's any chance at all" and "if it's more likely than not" are very different people, and most of us have never said which we are.
3. **Is it reversible?** Can we undo this if we're wrong, and at what cost? Most people's real rule turns out to be about reversibility, not probability — and that's a rule two people can often agree on even when their instincts differ.
4. **Who eats the cost of being wrong in each direction?** If we act and it was unnecessary, who pays? If we wait and it was serious, who pays? Frequently these are different people, and that's the actual disagreement in disguise.

**A worked example.** Two people argue about a policy. Ask question 3 first. If it turns out one is thinking about something easily undone and the other is thinking about something permanent, they were never arguing about the same decision — and they'll often swap positions once that's clear.

**Where it ends:** if you both answer honestly and still differ, one of you fears the harm of doing nothing more than the harm of doing the wrong thing, and one of you is the reverse. That's a disposition. It shows up in how you drive, invest, and parent, and it isn't going to change tonight.

## CRUX 03 — When does my choice become your business?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** at what point does an individual decision affect enough other people that the group gets a say.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Some risks aren't personal. If what you do lands other people in hospital, or costs everyone money, it stopped being purely your call. Living together means accepting some limits."

> "Almost anything can be described as affecting others if you draw the circle wide enough. That reasoning has no natural stopping point, and it always ends with someone else deciding what you may do with your own life."

**Where it shows up:** mandates, helmet and seatbelt laws, smoking, drug policy, gun ownership, what your kid is allowed to do at someone else's house.

**The test — both people answer:**

1. **Name the limit you accept.** Everyone accepts some. Seatbelts, drunk driving, building codes. Which ones, and why those?
2. **Name the limit you reject.** Same exercise the other way. Someone who accepts every limit or rejects every limit is arguing a position they don't actually live.
3. **What's the difference between the two you just named?** This is the real question. Almost everyone has a line. Very few have ever said out loud where it is, and hearing your own answer is usually the most useful thing in the session.
4. **Does it change if the numbers change?** If the harm to others were ten times larger, would your answer move? If it wouldn't, you're arguing a principle. If it would, you're arguing a threshold — and thresholds are negotiable in a way principles aren't.

**A worked example.** Two people are stuck on a mandate. Both do questions 1 and 2. One accepts drunk-driving laws but rejects vaccine mandates; the other accepts both. Question 3 surfaces the difference — usually something like directness of harm, or whether the risk is chosen. That's the seam. It's specific, it's honest, and neither person had to call the other reckless or authoritarian to get there.

**Where it ends:** if you've both named your line and they're in different places for reasons neither can dislodge, you disagree about how much weight to give one person's autonomy against a diffuse cost to everyone. Political philosophy has not settled this in three hundred years. You will not settle it before the food gets cold.

## CRUX 04 — What do we owe later?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** how much present cost is worth bearing for a future benefit — and how confident we have to be about the future to justify it.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Some problems compound. Dealing with them later costs vastly more than dealing with them now, and the people who pay later mostly don't get a vote today. Deferring is a way of passing the bill to someone who can't object."

> "Predictions about the far future are unreliable, and present costs are certain. People suffer now, concretely, so that a projected benefit might arrive for someone else. Wealth and capability compound too — a richer future may solve this more cheaply than we can."

**Where it shows up:** climate spending, government debt, pensions, infrastructure, saving vs. spending, whether to push a kid hard now for a payoff later.

**The test — both people answer:**

1. **How far out do you actually plan?** In your own life — mortgage, retirement, your kids. People are often far more patient privately than the position they argue publicly, and noticing the gap is instructive.
2. **How much does a benefit thirty years out count against one today?** Half? A tenth? Nothing? You've never said. Say it now. Two people with the same facts and different answers here will never converge, and it takes one sentence to find out.
3. **What's your confidence in the projection?** Not whether the direction is right — how much the specific number matters to your answer. If your position doesn't change when the projection is halved, the projection isn't what's driving you.
4. **Who's paying, and who's benefiting?** If they're the same people, this is a savings question. If they're different people, it's a fairness question, and those don't resolve the same way.

**A worked example.** Two people argue about debt. Both answer question 2 with a rough number. One is at "a dollar in thirty years is worth about ten cents today," the other at "about eighty cents." Every downstream disagreement follows from that gap, and it took thirty seconds to find. Neither number is wrong.

**Where it ends:** if your discount rates genuinely differ, you disagree about how much the future counts. That's as close to bedrock as this gets. Say it plainly and go get dessert.

## CRUX 05 — Who actually knows?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether trained expertise or direct personal experience gives you a better read on a situation.

**How each side would put it:**

> "People who've studied something for twenty years see patterns across thousands of cases. Your experience is one data point, and you can't see what you don't know. That's not an insult — it's true of everyone including me."

> "Experts see the version of the problem that reaches them, filtered through categories they were taught. The person living it notices things that never make it into the data because nobody thought to measure them. Being trained in something also trains you not to see certain things."

**Where it shows up:** medicine and symptoms doctors dismiss, education policy vs. teachers, economic statistics vs. what groceries cost you, city planning, addiction, anything where someone says "with respect, I actually live here."

**The test — run on both:**

1. **What would this source *not* be in a position to notice?** Every vantage point has a blind spot. The expert can't see what never got recorded. The person living it can't see whether their case is typical. Both answers exist. Say them.
2. **Is this a claim about what happens, or about how often it happens?** Lived experience is strong evidence that something *can* happen and often the only evidence it happens at all. It's weak evidence about frequency. Most arguments here confuse the two.
3. **Has the expert view changed, and did it change because of people reporting experience?** Often yes — that's evidence the channel works. Sometimes it took decades. That's evidence it works slowly.
4. **Which complaint is this?** "They got it wrong" and "they were dismissive of me" are different grievances, and one sentence usually carries both. Someone leaves an appointment with a correct diagnosis and no follow-up questions asked, and later says *doctors don't know anything about this.* The diagnosis may have been right and the encounter may still have been bad. Ask which one is being claimed. If you argue the knowledge question when the grievance was about the encounter, you can win completely and change nothing — you answered something they weren't asking. Only the first complaint is what this crux can settle.

**A worked example.** Someone says a treatment didn't work for them; someone else quotes the trial data. Question 2 resolves it in a sentence — the trial says how often it works, the person says it didn't work for them, and both are true simultaneously. The actual disagreement is usually about what should happen next, not about either fact.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that expertise is better on frequency and experience is better on existence, and you still differ, you disagree about which error is worse: dismissing a real effect because it's rare, or chasing a false one because it's vivid. That's a temperament, and it's usually formed by something that happened to you.

## CRUX 06 — Is it bad, or is it getting better?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether to judge a situation by where it stands right now or by which direction it's moving.

**How each side would put it:**

> "The number is still unacceptable. Telling me it's improved doesn't help the people affected today, and 'better than it was' has been used to justify not finishing the job for as long as there have been jobs to finish."

> "Direction is the only thing you can actually steer. If something has improved for thirty years, whatever we're doing is working, and tearing it up because the level offends you risks reversing the one thing that's going right."

**Where it shows up:** crime, poverty, emissions, healthcare wait times, wages, your kid's grades. Your own example — one person says spending is $X billion, the other says it's the best it's ever been. Both are true and they aren't arguing about the same thing.

**The test:**

1. **Put both numbers on the table.** Current level *and* the trend over a stated window. Refuse to proceed until you have both. This alone ends a surprising share of these arguments, because each person was holding one and assuming the other was in dispute.
2. **Who picked the start date?** Trends are enormously sensitive to where you begin. Try three different start points and see if the story survives. If it doesn't, the trend claim is weaker than it sounded.
3. **Compared to what?** Better than last year, better than peer countries, better than a reasonable target — three different comparisons that give three different verdicts. Name which one you're using.
4. **What would count as good enough?** If the answer is "no level would be acceptable while anyone is affected," that's a moral position, not an empirical one — which is fine, but say it, because no amount of data will move it.

**A worked example.** Both sides pull the actual series. It has improved 40% over twenty years and is still worse than every comparable country. Neither person was lying, both had a real point, and the remaining disagreement is question 4 — whether "improving" earns patience. That's a much smaller, more honest argument than the one they were having.

**Where it ends:** if the numbers are agreed and you still differ, one of you weights urgency for people affected now, the other weights not disrupting something that's working. Say that out loud and you're done.

## CRUX 07 — Does it matter what they meant?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether to judge a decision, policy, or person by the intention behind it or by what actually resulted.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Results are what people live with. Good intentions don't feed anyone, and treating them as a defence is how bad policy survives review after review. Judge the outcome."

> "You can't run a society where people are condemned for consequences they couldn't have foreseen. Nobody would ever act. Intent is how we distinguish a mistake from wrongdoing, and it's the basis of every legal system we have."

**Where it shows up:** policy post-mortems, apologies and whether they count, workplace conflict, discipline with kids, historical figures, anything where "that's not what I meant" gets said.

**The test:**

1. **Was the outcome foreseeable at the time, with what was known then?** This is the question that does most of the work, and it's often answerable. Not "did they know" but "could they reasonably have known."
2. **What did they do when the result became clear?** This separates the two positions more cleanly than the original decision does. Almost everyone agrees that persisting after the outcome is visible is a different thing from causing it unknowingly.
3. **Are we deciding what to do next, or assigning blame for what happened?** These need different rules and get run together constantly. For the first, intent barely matters. For the second, it's central.
4. **Would you apply this standard to someone on your own side?** Asked in both directions. The answer is frequently no, and noticing that is worth more than winning the point.

**A worked example.** An argument about a policy that failed. Question 3 first: are we deciding whether to keep it, or whether the people who made it were fools? If it's the first, intent is irrelevant and the argument is much simpler. Half of these disputes are two people answering different questions.

**Where it ends:** if you agree on foreseeability and response and still differ, one of you thinks a functioning society has to protect good-faith actors from consequences they didn't intend, and the other thinks that protection is what lets harm continue. Both of those beliefs are held by reasonable people for reasons rooted in things they've seen.

## CRUX 08 — The average or the story?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether the reliable picture comes from aggregate data or from specific cases.

**How each side would put it:**

> "One story tells you nothing about how common something is. The plural of anecdote isn't data, and building policy on the case that made the news is how you get expensive responses to rare problems."

> "Averages hide everything that matters. They smooth away the people at the edges, and those people are often the entire point. A statistic that says things are fine on average is compatible with a catastrophe for a specific group."

**Where it shows up:** crime and one incident, healthcare and one denied claim, immigration, school performance, side effects, "the economy is strong" versus how people describe their own finances.

**The test:**

1. **Is the disagreement about frequency or about severity?** Aggregates answer the first well. Cases answer the second well. Nearly every argument here is one person answering each.
2. **Does the average hide a split?** Pull the distribution if it exists. Frequently the average is fine and two subgroups are having completely different experiences — in which case both people were right and neither had the full picture.
3. **How did each of you encounter this?** A story reaches you because it was selected for being striking. A statistic reaches you through a chain of choices about what to measure. Neither arrives neutral. Name the path.
4. **What would change your mind — a number or a case?** If one of you would only accept data and the other only accepts a person's account, that's your seam and it's a good one to hit early.

**A worked example.** One person cites a rate, the other tells a story about someone they know. Question 2: is there a breakdown by region, age, income? Often yes, and often it shows the storyteller's group is genuinely an outlier. Now both facts are on the table and the argument becomes what to do about it — which was the real question all along.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept the rate and the case as true, you disagree about how much a rare severe harm should weigh against a common mild one. There's no formula. Economists have argued about it for a century and they're also not finished.

## CRUX 09 — Whose fault is it?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether an outcome is best explained by the choices individuals made or by the conditions they were making them in.

**How each side would put it:**

> "People in the same situation get different results, which means choices matter. Explaining everything by circumstance takes away the one thing a person can actually control, and telling someone their effort is irrelevant is not kindness."

> "If a pattern holds across millions of people, it isn't millions of independent character flaws — it's the situation doing the work. Judging each case individually means never noticing the thing producing all of them."

**Where it shows up:** poverty, addiction, obesity, crime, school outcomes, business failure, your kid's screentime. Almost every social argument is secretly this one.

**The test:**

1. **Is the claim about one person or about a pattern?** Both can be true at once — someone made bad choices *and* the odds were arranged against them. Most fights here are one person talking about a case and the other about a rate.
2. **What changes if you change the conditions?** Where a place changed a policy and the numbers moved, that's evidence conditions matter. Where they didn't move, that's evidence too. This is checkable more often than people expect.
3. **What's the claim actually being used for?** "This person is responsible" and "this is where we should intervene" don't have to agree. You can hold someone accountable and still think the leverage is somewhere else.
4. **Same standard both directions?** Does the person emphasizing circumstance apply it to people they disapprove of? Does the person emphasizing choice apply it to their own successes? Answer honestly. Almost nobody passes this cleanly.

**A worked example.** An argument about why a group is struggling. Question 3 first: are you two disagreeing about who deserves blame, or about what would fix it? Those come apart immediately, and people who can't agree on the first often agree readily on the second.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that choices and conditions each contribute, you disagree about the mix, and about which error costs more — excusing someone who could have done better, or blaming someone who couldn't. Neither of you will produce a number for that.

## CRUX 10 — Who has to prove what?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** what the default is when nobody can prove anything, and therefore who loses the argument by failing to make their case.

**How each side would put it:**

> "The people proposing a change carry the burden. Things as they are have at least been tested by time. If you can't show the change is an improvement, we don't make it."

> "The current arrangement isn't neutral — it was chosen by someone, often for reasons that no longer apply. Treating it as the default that needs no defence just protects whoever benefits from it now."

**Where it shows up:** new regulations, drug approvals, changing a tradition, altering a family routine, product safety, whether an unproven claim gets treated as false or as open.

**The test:**

1. **Say the default out loud.** "If we can't resolve this, what happens?" Whoever the answer favours is not carrying the burden. Naming it is usually enough to reveal that you were arguing about the default, not the evidence.
2. **Is the current state actually the tested one?** Sometimes yes — it's been running for fifty years. Sometimes it was introduced recently with no more evidence than the proposal. Check which.
3. **What does being wrong cost in each direction?** Burden of proof is really a bet about which mistake you'd rather make. Say which mistake you're more afraid of.
4. **Do you apply this default to changes you like?** Everyone demands rigour from proposals they dislike and waves it through for ones they favour. Check yourself before you check them.

**A worked example.** Two people argue about a proposed rule, one demanding evidence it works, the other demanding evidence it's harmful. Question 1 exposes that they never disagreed about the evidence at all — there isn't any — they disagree about who that absence favours. That's the whole argument, and it took one question.

**Where it ends:** if you both name the default and still differ, one of you fears breaking something that works and the other fears preserving something that doesn't. That's a disposition toward change itself and it's remarkably stable across topics — which is useful to know about each other.

## CRUX 11 — When is it force?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether pressure short of an actual mandate counts as coercion.

**How each side would put it:**

> "Nobody was forced. There was no law, no penalty, no one was arrested. If you could say no and accept the consequences, that's a choice, even if it was a hard one."

> "A choice between compliance and losing your job isn't a choice in any way that matters. Calling it voluntary because there's no statute is a technicality that lets people apply enormous pressure and deny doing it."

**Where it shows up:** employment conditions, vaccine and testing requirements, social pressure, contracts, plea bargains, parenting — "he chose to, I just explained what would happen otherwise."

**The test:**

1. **What was the cost of saying no, concretely?** Not "there were consequences" — name them. A raised eyebrow and losing your income are both consequences and nobody thinks they're the same.
2. **Where's your line?** Everyone has one. A fine is force to almost nobody; losing your home is force to almost everyone. Say roughly where yours sits, then compare.
3. **Who applied the pressure — the state, an employer, or other people?** Many disagreements are only about this. Two people can fully agree that a pressure was severe and disagree entirely about whether the source has the standing to apply it.
4. **Would you call it force if the pressure ran the other way?** The most reliable check in this whole crux. Try it before you answer the others.

**A worked example.** An argument about a workplace requirement. Both answer question 2 with a rough threshold. One says force begins at any material financial penalty, the other at physical compulsion. Every downstream disagreement follows from that gap, and neither position is absurd.

**Where it ends:** if your thresholds differ and hold up under question 4, you disagree about how much pressure a free choice can absorb before it stops being free. That's a genuine philosophical divide with a long, unresolved literature behind it.

## CRUX 12 — Does everyone agreeing mean anything?

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether broad agreement among qualified people is evidence that something is true, or evidence about how that group forms opinions.

**How each side would put it:**

> "When people who've studied something independently converge on an answer, that convergence is information. It's not proof, but a lone dissenter isn't automatically the brave truth-teller — usually they're just wrong, which is why we notice the rare exceptions."

> "Consensus can form because everyone read the same three papers, trained under the same people, and needs the same funding. Agreement measured inside a group that shares those things measures the group as much as the world."

**Where it shows up:** climate, nutrition, economics, medicine, any argument containing "the science is settled" or "science isn't done by consensus."

**The test:**

1. **Did they arrive independently?** Different methods, different countries, different funding, different decades — that's convergence. Everyone citing the same original study is one data point wearing a crowd costume. Check which one this is.
2. **What happens to a dissenter in this field?** Are there published disagreements, or is the field visibly quiet? Some fields argue in public constantly. That's a health indicator either way.
3. **Has this consensus been overturned before, and how did it go?** Look at the actual history of the field. Some have reversed repeatedly, some haven't reversed in a century. That's checkable and it's rarely checked.
4. **What's the specific claim?** Consensus is usually strong on a narrow core and much weaker on the policy attached to it. People argue as though agreeing with one commits you to the other. Separate them.

**A worked example.** An argument about a scientific claim. Question 4 does the work: one person is defending the core finding, the other is attacking the policy built on top of it. Both are often right. The consensus fight was a proxy.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that consensus is real evidence and also socially produced, you disagree about the weight — which is a judgment about how much this particular field's incentives distort it. That's not resolvable tonight, but it's a much narrower disagreement than the one you started with.

## CRUX 13 — Being right and being decent

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether treating someone respectfully is a separate obligation from getting the facts right, or whether it's part of the same job.

**How each side would put it:**

> "The truth doesn't change based on how it's delivered. Requiring every correct statement to be gentle is how uncomfortable facts get suppressed, and softening a message often costs the person the information they needed."

> "How you say it determines whether it's heard, so delivery isn't decoration — it's whether the truth arrives at all. And someone can be entirely correct and still treat you as an idiot, which is a real harm independent of the facts."

**Where it shows up:** medical appointments, feedback at work, arguments with teenagers, public debate, and — reliably — inside every other crux in this library, where "you're wrong" and "you're not listening to me" arrive in the same sentence.

**The test:**

1. **Split the sentence.** Ask directly: is the complaint that the conclusion is wrong, or that the exchange was bad? Both can be true. They need different responses and get conflated constantly.
2. **Would being gentler have changed the facts?** If no, delivery is a separate conversation — worth having, but not this one.
3. **Would being gentler have changed whether they heard it?** If yes, delivery is part of the job and the person who was right still failed at something.
4. **Ask yourself:** in this argument, do I want to be understood or do I want to be right? Answer privately and truthfully. It changes what you say next more than any other question here.

**A worked example.** Two people re-litigate an argument they had a month ago. Question 1 reveals one of them has been defending a fact and the other has been describing an insult. Neither has responded to the other in four weeks.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept that correctness and decency are separate obligations, you disagree about what's owed when they conflict. That's a question about what conversation is *for* — and it may be the most useful thing your group ever names out loud, because it governs every other argument you have.
