title: "Is it bad, or is it getting better?"
type: evidence

**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether to judge a situation by where it stands right now or by which direction it's moving.

**How each side would put it:**

> "The number is still unacceptable. Telling me it's improved doesn't help the people affected today, and 'better than it was' has been used to justify not finishing the job for as long as there have been jobs to finish."

> "Direction is the only thing you can actually steer. If something has improved for thirty years, whatever we're doing is working, and tearing it up because the level offends you risks reversing the one thing that's going right."

**Where it shows up:** crime, poverty, emissions, healthcare wait times, wages, your kid's grades. Your own example — one person says spending is $X billion, the other says it's the best it's ever been. Both are true and they aren't arguing about the same thing.

**The test:**

1. **Put both numbers on the table.** Current level *and* the trend over a stated window. Refuse to proceed until you have both. This alone ends a surprising share of these arguments, because each person was holding one and assuming the other was in dispute.
2. **Who picked the start date?** Trends are enormously sensitive to where you begin. Try three different start points and see if the story survives. If it doesn't, the trend claim is weaker than it sounded.
3. **Compared to what?** Better than last year, better than peer countries, better than a reasonable target — three different comparisons that give three different verdicts. Name which one you're using.
4. **What would count as good enough?** If the answer is "no level would be acceptable while anyone is affected," that's a moral position, not an empirical one — which is fine, but say it, because no amount of data will move it.

**A worked example.** Both sides pull the actual series. It has improved 40% over twenty years and is still worse than every comparable country. Neither person was lying, both had a real point, and the remaining disagreement is question 4 — whether "improving" earns patience. That's a much smaller, more honest argument than the one they were having.

**Where it ends:** if the numbers are agreed and you still differ, one of you weights urgency for people affected now, the other weights not disrupting something that's working. Say that out loud and you're done.
