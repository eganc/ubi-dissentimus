**What you're really disagreeing about:** whether the reliable picture comes from aggregate data or from specific cases.

**How each side would put it:**

> "One story tells you nothing about how common something is. The plural of anecdote isn't data, and building policy on the case that made the news is how you get expensive responses to rare problems."

> "Averages hide everything that matters. They smooth away the people at the edges, and those people are often the entire point. A statistic that says things are fine on average is compatible with a catastrophe for a specific group."

**Where it shows up:** crime and one incident, healthcare and one denied claim, immigration, school performance, side effects, "the economy is strong" versus how people describe their own finances.

**The test:**

1. **Is the disagreement about frequency or about severity?** Aggregates answer the first well. Cases answer the second well. Nearly every argument here is one person answering each.
2. **Does the average hide a split?** Pull the distribution if it exists. Frequently the average is fine and two subgroups are having completely different experiences — in which case both people were right and neither had the full picture.
3. **How did each of you encounter this?** A story reaches you because it was selected for being striking. A statistic reaches you through a chain of choices about what to measure. Neither arrives neutral. Name the path.
4. **What would change your mind — a number or a case?** If one of you would only accept data and the other only accepts a person's account, that's your seam and it's a good one to hit early.

**A worked example.** One person cites a rate, the other tells a story about someone they know. Question 2: is there a breakdown by region, age, income? Often yes, and often it shows the storyteller's group is genuinely an outlier. Now both facts are on the table and the argument becomes what to do about it — which was the real question all along.

**Where it ends:** if you both accept the rate and the case as true, you disagree about how much a rare severe harm should weigh against a common mild one. There's no formula. Economists have argued about it for a century and they're also not finished.
