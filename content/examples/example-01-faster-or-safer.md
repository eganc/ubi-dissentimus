*Adapted from a real exchange between two friends. Names, places and identifying details have been changed. The positions are theirs. The session steps and sealed answers were written afterwards to show how the structure would have handled it.*

## Where it started

Dana and Sam have argued about politics by text for years. The pattern was settled: one said something sharp, the other answered the sharpness, and nothing moved.

This time the argument narrowed to one question: how their country should handle the race to build advanced AI.

**Sam's position, in Sam's terms:**

> "This is a wartime situation. A rival power is building the same thing as fast as it can. Every layer of regulation slows us down, and falling behind is the real danger. The stakes are about as high as they get."

**Dana's position, in Dana's terms:**

> "The technology is dangerous whoever builds it. The race has no finish line — being ahead today only sets the pace for tomorrow. Speeding up a dangerous thing doesn't make it less dangerous."

One thing was sitting in the thread unremarked. Both of them had said, in writing, that advanced AI is a serious threat, possibly an existential one. That was never in dispute. Neither had said so to the other.

## The first wording

Dana proposed the claim: *Institutions are more trustworthy than individuals.*

It was a considered guess. The argument kept circling back to whether to back a decisive leader or a slow process, and that looked like the thing underneath.

Sam did not accept it. "That's not what I think. I'd trust a government to win this. I want it to move faster."

This is what the claim step is for. A wording written by one person tends to describe how that person sees the argument. It may be accurate. It still has to be accepted by the other side before anyone answers it.

## The claim

After two revisions, both accepted this wording:

> Moving faster on advanced AI makes the outcome safer than moving slowly.

Asked whether this was something they could look up, they chose the second option: they could agree on every fact and still disagree. So the private questions were the ones for values — *Where's your line?* and *What would have to be different for your answer to change?*

## The sealed answers

Neither could see the other's answers until both were in.

**Sam — where's your line?**

> "If the rival had no serious program and was years behind, I'd slow down and put safety rules in first. Speed is only worth it because the race is real."

**Sam — what would have to be different?**

> "If slowing down were mutual — both sides at once, and we could verify the other side was doing it — I'd take that deal."

**Dana — where's your line?**

> "If we knew the other side would build it carelessly and use it against us, I'd accept racing to get there first. That's the case where speed buys safety."

**Dana — what would have to be different?**

> "If being ahead meant you could stop — if there were a point where the lead was safe to hold — the race would make sense to me. I don't see that point."

## What the reveal showed

Read side by side, the answers are closer than the argument was. Both would race against a careless rival. Both would slow down if slowing down were mutual and could be checked. Neither was arguing that speed is good or that safety doesn't matter.

What separated them was underneath the claim, in two places:

1. **How likely is the dangerous case?** Sam treats a careless, capable rival as the situation they are already in. Dana treats it as possible but not established.
2. **Can a lead be cashed in?** Sam assumes that being ahead gives you the leverage to set terms later. Dana assumes a lead in this race has to be defended forever and never becomes safety.

They chose: *We disagree about something underneath this.*

## Going down a level

They tried to name the second one as a claim.

First attempt: *Being ahead lets you stop.* The gate returned it: that can't be checked as written.

Second attempt: *A country that gets well ahead will use the lead to slow down rather than keep going.* Also not checkable. It describes a future neither of them can observe, and the historical cases each would reach for point in different directions depending on which ones you count.

Two failures at the gate is a result, not an error.

## Where it ends

They reached the bottom. Dana and Sam disagree about whether a lead in this race can ever be turned into safety, and neither can name an observation that would settle it.

Sam's view, intact: the danger of coming second is concrete and close, and a lead is the only leverage anyone has ever had.

Dana's view, intact: in a race with no finish line, a lead is only the pace for the next lap, and the danger does not change with who holds it.

Each of them thinks the other's mistake is the one that can't be undone. That is the shape of the crux *Do you act before you're sure?* — one of them fears the cost of waiting more, the other fears the cost of the wrong move more. It's a disposition, and it isn't going to change tonight.

## What the structure did

1. It surfaced an agreement neither had stated: the technology is dangerous.
2. It declined a wording one person wrote for both, and made room for one both could accept.
3. It moved the argument from who should be trusted to a single question neither can settle by looking something up. Nobody had to lose.

Next time Dana and Sam argue about this, they will end up here again. Knowing that is the point.
